## 1. Editing on a PC with integrated graphics only (editors, cheapest set-up, proxy/browser/phone workflows)

### Takeaway
Only CapCut could be checked. Its free tier is reported to export up to 1080p (enough for all four platforms), but two things have changed that a newcomer misses: the US list price of Pro is now reported at $19.99/month or $179.99/year, and the US terms (updated 15 April 2026) run through the TikTok USDS Joint Venture and take a broad, perpetual, sub-licensable licence over user content. Requirements and prices for DaVinci Resolve, Clipchamp, Canva, Descript, Instagram's Edits and open-source editors were **not verified** this session.

### Inferences
- A 1080p export cap is not a real limitation for this creator: X plays 720p for free accounts and at most 1080p for paid ones, and Reels/Shorts are specified around 1080×1920 (see Q2 and Q4). Paying for an editor tier just to unlock 4K export buys nothing on these platforms.
- The CapCut cost a newcomer should budget is not "about $8" (the figure still repeated in older guides) but potentially $19.99/month in the US; the free tier plus avoidance of Pro-tagged effects is the lowest-cost route, subject to the unresolved watermark question.
- The CapCut licence clause matters most for client or brand work and for unreleased original characters: anything uploaded to the editor is licensed broadly to the operator. An offline open-source editor avoids that exposure entirely. (Inference from the terms above; not legal advice.)
- Never publish with an editor's or another platform's watermark: X's Video Kickstart Bonus explicitly disqualifies videos carrying other platforms' watermarks (cited in Q6), so the clean master should come out of the editor, not be re-downloaded from TikTok or Instagram.
- (Inference resting partly on unverified background, see Gaps.) AI clips are short (seconds each) and usually 720p–1080p H.264, which is light to decode; assembling 5–20 of them with captions and music is within reach of integrated graphics in a lightweight editor. The hardware limit bites on AI effects, upscaling, frame interpolation and heavy grading — steps better done in the cloud or on a phone. Proxy editing makes the timeline responsive but does not shorten the final render.

## 2. Resolution and upscaling (what platforms keep, whether 4K is pointless, upscaling and frame-interpolation options)

### Takeaway
For X, 4K is pointless: X's own documentation says unsubscribed accounts get 720p playback and paid accounts top out at 1080p, with a recommended upload bitrate of only 5–8 Mbps. Reels are specified at 1080×1920, and sources disagree on whether Shorts keeps more than 1080p. Topaz is now subscription-only (Topaz Video Personal: $59/month with no commitment, or $299/year billed annually) and Adobe announced on 25 June 2026 that it is acquiring the company. Built-in generator upscalers, cheap cloud upscalers and frame interpolation could not be researched.

- X developer documentation: "unsubscribed users can upload a 720p video and get a 720p playback", while higher-tier (paid) accounts can upload 1080p; recommended resolutions are 1280×720 (landscape), 720×1280 (portrait) and 720×720 (square); recommended codec H.264 High Profile at 30 or 60 fps, minimum video bitrate 5,000 kbps, audio AAC-LC at 128 kbps or more. ### Inferences
- Target master for all four platforms: **1080×1920 (9:16)**. On X, whether viewers see 720p or 1080p is decided by the uploader's subscription status, not by the render — so for an X-first creator the entry paid X plan (reported at $8/month, see Q4) improves delivered quality more than any upscaler, and 4K export or 4K upscaling adds cost and render time for no visible gain on X.
- Upscaling is worth paying for only in a narrow case: the generator outputs 720p (or lower) and the creator has 1080p playback on the destination (paid X, Reels, TikTok, Shorts). Paying to go from 1080p to 4K is not justified for these platforms on the evidence found.
- Topaz at $59/month (or $299/year) is out of proportion to a lowest-cost short-video workflow, and its strength is local processing, which (per unverified background below) needs a dedicated GPU the user does not have.

### Inferences
- The $8 versus $10 Suno discrepancy is probably annual-billing versus monthly-billing pricing of the same Pro plan (the pre-settlement price list had exactly that pair); treat it as "about $10/month, less if billed annually" until checked.
- A newcomer should assume that free-tier AI music is not usable in monetised or commercial videos, and should not assume that upgrading later makes earlier free-tier tracks commercial (that was the rule in Suno's earlier terms — unverified for the September 2026 terms).
- If Suno's 20/60-downloads-per-month cap is real, it is still enough for a solo creator publishing a few videos a week; the cost that matters is the subscription, not the cap.
- The label settlements reach ordinary users through product changes (download caps, disabled downloads, model retirements), not through liability aimed at them.

  - Lip-sync is offered inside several generators and by specialist tools; open-source lip-sync needs a GPU. It only matters for talking characters; stylised 3D characters with narration avoid it entirely.
  - Captions: Instagram, TikTok and YouTube all add automatic captions in-app at no cost; Edits and Clipchamp had free auto-captions; CapCut had moved part of auto-captioning behind Pro; DaVinci Resolve's transcription was Studio-only. ## 4. Platform specifications as of November 2026 (X by tier; essentials for Reels, TikTok, Shorts; how X ranks video)

### Takeaway
On X, a free account is limited to 140 seconds, 512 MB and 720p playback; any paid plan lifts this to 1080p and multi-hour uploads, but X's own help pages currently disagree on the paid ceiling (about 3 hours/8 GB on two pages, 4 hours/16 GB on another) and Android is capped at 10 minutes. X's For You ranking code is public and models video-watch signals, but the live weights are not. One 1080×1920 H.264 MP4 under 140 seconds works on all four platforms; Shorts must be 3 minutes or less and Reels over 3 minutes are not recommended to new audiences. TikTok's upload limits were not verified.

### Inferences
- A single master that is safe everywhere: 1080×1920, 9:16, H.264 High Profile MP4, 30 fps (or the generator's native 24 fps), about 8 Mbps video (X recommends 5–8 Mbps, minimum 5), AAC at 128 kbps or more, **under 140 seconds and 512 MB** so it also uploads from a free X account. (Derived from X's published specs, the strictest of the four found.)
- For this creator's short clips, the only X limits that bite on a free account are 720p playback and exclusion from monetisation; length and size are irrelevant. The cheapest paid plan (reported $8/month on the web, more through app stores) is what unlocks 1080p.
- Upload natively to X rather than linking to YouTube or TikTok, and design for watch-through: the public ranking code predicts watch and dwell actions on native media. This is the best-supported ranking advice; the specific multipliers circulating in blogs are not reliable.
- X is investing in short vertical video (vertical ad format, a 9:16 bonus pilot), so 9:16 is a sensible default on X as well as on the other three.
- Length is a strategic choice with cost consequences: X and Reels work with short clips, Shorts allows up to 3 minutes, but TikTok only pays on videos over one minute — which for AI video means stitching many more generated clips (more credits) per post.

## 5. AI-content disclosure (X, Meta, TikTok, YouTube; automatic detection; penalties; EU AI Act Article 50)

### Takeaway
Every platform ties *mandatory* disclosure to **realistic** content: stylised, animated or fantastical 3D is exempt on YouTube and (by its examples) TikTok, Meta's rule targets photorealistic video and realistic-sounding audio, and X's "Made with AI" label is a voluntary toggle except for AI videos of armed conflict posted by monetised creators. Platforms increasingly label automatically from C2PA metadata, invisible watermarks and their own classifiers, whatever the creator ticks. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act has applied since 2 August 2026 and was not postponed; only the providers' machine-readable-marking duty for systems already on the market got a grace period, to 2 December 2026. For a creator, the operative duty is to disclose deepfakes.

### Inferences
- A creator making **stylised** 3D or cartoon content is, on the evidence found, outside the mandatory-label rules of YouTube, TikTok and Meta and outside X's one mandatory case. A creator making **photorealistic** 3D (realistic people, places, products, events) is inside them on YouTube, TikTok and Meta. "3D-looking" is therefore not one category for labelling purposes — the realism of the result decides.
- Auto-labels can appear regardless of the creator's choice, because platforms read provenance metadata and watermarks or run classifiers. The safe assumption is that platforms can tell; an undisclosed realistic AI video risks removal, restriction or loss of monetisation, while a disclosed one carries no stated reach penalty (YouTube, per secondary sources). Voluntary labelling is cheap insurance.
- On X the label is a one-tap toggle and currently optional for ordinary content; using it costs nothing and removes the risk if the leaked "mandatory" rule ever materialises.
- EU position for an individual creator, combining the cited text with the unverified background below: the heavy Article 50 duty (machine-readable marking) falls on the tool provider, not the creator; the creator's own duty is to disclose **deepfakes**. A clearly stylised cartoon is unlikely to meet the Act's deepfake definition; realistic depictions of real people, places or events are. Whether a monetising solo creator falls outside the Act's "purely personal, non-professional" carve-out is unsettled — a creator earning from content should assume the duty applies. This is inference, not legal advice.
- Because providers must have marking in place by 2 December 2026, generator output will increasingly carry provenance signals from December 2026 onward, making platform auto-labelling more common, not less.
- Regional summary: EU/EEA creators have a statutory deepfake-disclosure duty on top of platform rules and see extra "Made with AI" indicators on X; for the UK and US no creator-side labelling statute was found in this session (not researched), so platform rules are what applies in practice.

  - Google embeds its SynthID invisible watermark in Veo/Imagen/Lyria output and offers a detector; OpenAI's Sora output carried a visible watermark plus C2PA metadata; C2PA metadata is usually lost when a clip is re-encoded in an editor, which is why platforms are adding invisible watermarks and classifiers.
  - Meta renamed its label from "Made with AI" to "AI info" in July 2024 after photographers complained about minor edits being tagged, and in September 2024 moved the label into the post menu for content that was only AI-edited.
  - Outside the EU: China's AI-content labelling measures took effect on 1 September 2025; California's AI Transparency Act (SB 942, as amended by AB 853) became operative on 2 August 2026 for large generative-AI providers, with platform duties following in 2027. Neither places a general labelling duty on an individual creator abroad, but they explain why tools and platforms label by default.

- **Unverified background (researcher's prior knowledge, ≤ mid-2026, NOT checked this session — every item needs verification):**
  - *United States — ownership.* The Copyright Office's report "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability" (29 January 2025) concluded that purely AI-generated material is not copyrightable, that prompts alone do not give the user enough control to be the author of the output, and that human contributions — creative selection, arrangement, editing and modification of AI output, plus human-written scripts, human-recorded voice and human-made music — remain protectable, case by case. Registration applicants must disclose and disclaim more-than-minimal AI-generated material (guidance of March 2023). In Thaler v. Perlmutter the D.C. Circuit held in March 2025 that the Copyright Act requires a human author, and the Supreme Court is believed to have declined to hear the case in March 2026. Verify at `copyright.gov/ai`.
  - *Practical consequence (inference from the above).* A finished video is likely protected as to the creator's script, edit and human-made audio, but the raw AI clips inside it probably are not — so others may be able to reuse the bare clips, and the creator should keep project files and drafts as evidence of human authorship.
  - *European Union.* No EU statute addresses authorship of AI output; the Court of Justice's originality test ("the author's own intellectual creation", reflecting free and creative human choices) points to no protection for purely AI-generated output and possible protection for AI-assisted work with sufficient human creative choices; a Prague court denied protection to an AI-generated image in 2023. The AI Act does not deal with ownership of outputs.
  - *United Kingdom.* Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 gives authorship of a "computer-generated" work with no human author to the person who made the arrangements necessary for its creation, with a 50-year term — an unusual protection that the government's December 2024 consultation proposed removing. A government report and impact assessment on copyright and AI was due by March 2026 under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025; its outcome was not checked. Verify at `gov.uk` (Intellectual Property Office).
  - *Tool terms — typical pitfalls.* Free tiers are often non-commercial and/or watermarked (seen above for Suno and Udio; also true at various times of several video generators and of ElevenLabs' free plan); upgrading later does not always convert earlier free-tier output to commercial use; many vendors take a broad licence to use prompts and outputs and show or reuse them publicly by default; some restrict ownership for larger companies; and terms almost always make the user responsible for infringement while offering indemnities only to enterprise customers. The generator-specific terms belong to the other researchers' scope and must be checked there.
  - *Style and characters.* Copyright does not protect a style as such, so a generic polished 3D-cartoon look is not infringing in itself; but specific characters and designs are protected, and studio names are trademarks — so prompting for or publishing recognisable characters, or marketing a video as "Pixar" or "Disney", invites takedowns, strikes and trademark or false-endorsement complaints. Describing the look (lighting, materials, proportions) rather than naming a studio is the standard precaution.
  - *Real people.* Using a real person's face or voice engages right-of-publicity and similar laws (for example Tennessee's ELVIS Act covering voice, and California and New York statutes), the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act (2025) for intimate deepfakes, platform impersonation rules, and in the EU the AI Act deepfake duty plus data-protection and national personality rights; the UK has no general image right, but passing-off and data-protection claims are possible. A federal NO FAKES Act had been reintroduced in 2025 but was not known to have passed; Denmark had proposed a likeness-protection law.
  - *Lawsuits.* Disney and Universal sued Midjourney in June 2025 and Warner Bros. Discovery followed in September 2025, both over generation of their characters; the three studios sued MiniMax (Hailuo) in September 2025; Disney licensed characters to OpenAI's Sora in December 2025 (the later fate of Sora and of that deal should be checked with the generator researcher); in Getty Images v. Stability AI the UK High Court largely rejected Getty's copyright claims in November 2025; the artists' class action Andersen v. Stability AI was still pending. These cases target vendors; their practical effect on users is tighter filters on characters and names, withdrawn models or features, and changed licence terms. Current status must be verified.

  - A Pew Research Center survey published in September 2025 found half of US adults more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, about three-quarters saying it is very important to be able to tell AI-made from human-made content, and about half not confident they could do so.
  - Industry surveys in 2025 (for example from the creator agency Billion Dollar Boy and the ad network Raptive) reported falling consumer enthusiasm for AI-made creator content and a marked drop in trust when viewers believe content is AI-generated; the exact figures were not re-checked.
  - YouTube's CEO said in his early-2026 annual letter that reducing low-quality, repetitive AI content was a priority; Pinterest added a "see fewer AI pins" control in late 2025; Meta's AI-video feed "Vibes" (September 2025) drew "slop" criticism.
  - Commentary on AI formats that did build audiences (recurring AI characters and series, comedic "vlog" formats, strongly art-directed shorts) consistently credits concept, writing, a consistent character or world, and sound design rather than the generator used. This is commentary, not measured evidence.
