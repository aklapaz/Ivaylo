## 1. EU AI Act Article 50 (transparency for AI-generated and manipulated content): status on 17 November 2026, provider versus deployer, the position of an individual creator, and the state of the Code of Practice and the Guidelines

### Takeaway
Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026 and no later law has postponed it: the AI Omnibus (in force 27 July 2026) delayed the high-risk rules and, for Article 50, only gave providers of generative AI systems already on the market until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking. The person who publishes content (the "deployer") must visibly label only deepfakes (and certain public-interest text); the Commission says a natural person acting in a purely personal capacity is outside the Act, but becomes a deployer once the activity brings "an economic benefit on a regular basis", and for deepfakes inside an evidently artistic or fictional work the duty shrinks to a non-intrusive disclosure. The voluntary Code of Practice (final 10 June 2026, judged adequate in July, 266 signatories on 16 November 2026) and the Commission Guidelines (published 20 July 2026) are both in place, with optional, free EU "AI" icons.

### Cited Findings

#### Status on 17 November 2026: in application, not delayed
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Decisive wording: "Article 50 of the AI Act applies as from 2 August 2026. From that date onwards, providers and deployers of AI systems must comply with the transparency obligations laid down in that provision. A limited grace period is envisaged only for AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 and only as regards the marking and detection obligation for AI-generated content (Article 50(2) of the AI Act). Providers of such systems must comply with those obligations only as from 2 December 2026." — [Commission Q&A: Transparency obligations under Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 29 Jul 2026]** The Commission's fact page states the grace period as enacted law: "Grace period for marking obligation until December 2026 for generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 (Article 50(2) AI Act, amended by AI Omnibus)." It adds: "Deepfakes generated before 2 August 2026: no mandatory retroactive labelling but encouraged." — [Quick facts: Transparency rules for AI systems](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/factpages/quick-facts-transparency-rules-ai-systems)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 19 Oct 2026]** The AI Omnibus "was adopted on 19 November 2025, a political agreement was reached on 7 May 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026"; the page links the final text in the Official Journal under reference `OJ:L_202601744`. The same page's application timeline says the AI Act "became applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions" and lists as exceptions only the earlier-applying prohibitions, AI-literacy and general-purpose-model rules and the postponed high-risk rules (2 December 2027 for Annex III uses, 2 August 2028 for AI in regulated products) — Article 50 is not among the exceptions. — [Commission: AI Act policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 31 Jul 2026]** The Commission's entry-into-force notice lists what the Omnibus changed: extended timelines for high-risk systems, relief for small mid-cap companies, a simpler AI-literacy duty, a ban on "nudification" apps, wider AI Office powers and procedural clarifications. It lists no postponement of the Article 50 transparency duties. — [AI Omnibus enters into force](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-omnibus-enters-force)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 20 Oct 2026]** Enforcement powers are live: "The enforcement powers of the AI Office and the national competent authorities of the Member States apply from 2 August 2026, when certain provisions of the AI Act become enforceable", and the list that follows includes "Transparency requirements for certain AI systems: For instance, chatbots shall inform people that they are interacting with AI (not an human), deepfakes shall be labelled, machine-readable marks shall be embedded in synthetic content." The page also says the new prohibitions on non-consensual intimate material and child sexual abuse material "apply from 2 December 2026". — [The enforcement framework of the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/enforcement-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 29 Jul 2026]** No retroactive duty: "AI-generated or manipulated outputs, including deep fakes falling within the scope of Article 50(2) and (4) of the AI Act which were generated and already made available before 2 August 2026, do not need to be marked or labelled retroactively." The same page says the date of application was not moved by the late publication of the Code: "From that date, all AI systems falling within the scope of that provision, placed on the EU market or put into service in the EU, must comply, regardless of when they were first placed on the market or put into service." (This page still describes the grace period with the words "If adopted", wording that predates the Omnibus's entry into force; the fact page quoted above supersedes it.) — [Commission Q&A: Signing the Code of Practice](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/signing-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | pages updated 19 Oct – 16 Nov 2026]** Commission pages updated in October and November 2026 keep describing the obligations as applicable since 2 August 2026 and mention no further delay: the Code page ("These transparency obligations, applicable from 2 August 2026"), the signatory list page and the digital-rulebook page. — [Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content); [Signatories page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/strong-backing-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content); [Digital rulebook page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/digital-rulebook)

#### What the provider of an AI tool must do
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Who is a provider: "natural or legal persons, public authorities, agencies or other bodies that develop AI systems, or have them developed, and place them on the EU market or put them into service under their own name or trademark", wherever they are based; "Providers of AI systems established or located outside the EU are also subject to the provisions of the AI Act if the output of their AI system is used in the EU." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** The marking duty (Article 50(2)): "providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, must ensure that AI-generated or manipulated content are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated." Providers of chatbots and other interactive systems must also make sure people are told they are dealing with AI (Article 50(1)). — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Outputs outside the marking duty, per the Guidelines as summarised by the Commission: "a short sequence of numbers, symbols or letters", "source code", machine-to-machine outputs never shown to humans, and "outputs that are only used in closed loop industrial and product development environments, for example for film production, unless they are the final output". The duty also "does not apply when the AI system performs an assistive function for standard editing". — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 29 Jul 2026]** Fact-page wording of the same duty: "Providers must apply a machine-readable mark to synthetic content generated or manipulated by AI and enable its detection, unless the AI system performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the input data or its semantics provided by the deployer." — [Quick facts](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/factpages/quick-facts-transparency-rules-ai-systems)

#### What the person who publishes (the "deployer") must do
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Definition: "Deployers of AI systems are natural or legal persons, public authorities, agencies or other bodies using AI systems under their authority, excluding use for personal, non-professional activities." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Duties: deployers "must ensure that they inform people when they use emotion recognition or biometric categorisation systems (Articles 50(3) of the AI Act) and clearly label deepfakes and AI-generated or manipulated text published on matters of public interest without human review or editorial control (Article 50(4) of the AI Act)." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 13 Oct 2026]** The duty is narrow: "Not all AI-generated or manipulated content needs to be labelled." Article 50(4) requires deployers to disclose (1) deepfakes and (2) "AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, which did not undergo human review or editorial control and where editorial responsibility was not assumed by any legal or natural person." — [EU icons for labelling AI-generated content](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Text exemption: "Published text that has undergone human review or editorial control – does not need to be labelled." Human review means "deliberate examination of the substance of the content by one or more natural persons possessing relevant knowledge and professional judgement"; "Superficial, solely formal, or procedural checks (e.g. spell-checking or grammatical correction) are not considered to be human review or editorial control"; "Editorial responsibility means that a person must hold the ultimate legal responsibility over the publication of the content". — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)

#### Individuals: the personal, non-professional exemption
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Decisive wording: "When a natural person uses an AI system in their personal capacity — for example, to generate deepfakes and disseminate them on social media — this is considered a personal activity. Such use is excluded from the scope of the AI Act. However, if it is an activity through which they gain an economic benefit on a regular basis (or are otherwise involved in a business, trade, occupational or freelance activity) this is considered a 'professional' activity. In this case, the natural person is considered to be a deployer of that AI system." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Employees and contractors: where the deployer is a company, "the individual employees that act under the instructions and under the control of that legal person (e.g. digital animators, web designers, content creators, journalists) should not be considered as separate deployers"; the company "remains a deployer even if third parties (e.g. contractors, freelancers) are involved in the operation of the system on its behalf and under its responsibility and control." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 29 Jul 2026]** The Code's deployer section is aimed at professional users: it can be signed by deployers "who use generative AI systems under their authority for professional purposes and are affected by the obligations concerning the disclosure of deepfakes". — [Commission Q&A: Signing the Code](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/signing-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content)

#### What counts as a deepfake, and the regime for artistic or fictional works
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Definition and test: deepfakes are "AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful." Three cumulative criteria must all be met: "Resemblance: a high level of similarity between the deepfake content and the simulated subject"; "Existing: simulated persons, objects, places, entities or events need to resemble someone or something that exists, can plausibly exist or could have plausibly existed in reality"; "False appearance to be authentic or truthful: this relates to the essential characteristic of deepfake content and its capacity to potentially deceive or mislead a person regarding the content's authenticity or truthfulness". — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Context and audience count. For the third criterion the Guidelines let deployers weigh "the level of resemblance", "the potential substantive message of the content", "the intended and foreseeable deployment contexts" and "the intended and reasonably foreseeable audience composition and their expectations". "If the intended audience within a specific deployment context does not expect the content to be authentic or truthful, the AI-generated or manipulated content may not falsely appear to be authentic or truthful. For example, the AI-generation or manipulation of background scenes, special effects, or technical pre- and post-processing as part of standard movie production processes are not likely to make content falsely appear to the audience to be authentic or truthful." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** Artistic and fictional works: "Some deepfakes are part of evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous works or programmes. For these, the transparency obligation is limited to the disclosure of the deepfake content in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 13 Oct 2026]** The icons page repeats the limited regime and also lists a law-enforcement exception ("authorised by law to detect, prevent, investigate or prosecute criminal offences"). In its table of examples, "Fully AI-composed music or art" carries the footnote "May enjoy limited disclosure requirements for artistic, creative, fictional or satirical works". — [EU icons page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)

#### How a deepfake must be disclosed: timing, form, and the EU icons
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** "Deployers must disclose deepfake content to a natural person upon first exposure at the latest. This disclosure should happen in a clear and distinguishable manner. It should be understandable and perceivable by natural persons (e.g. with visible or audible labels), without need for any specific technical tools or performing dedicated actions. Therefore, deployers cannot simply rely on the machine-readable marking embedded in the content by the provider under Article 50(2) of the AI Act to fulfil their disclosure obligation." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 13 Oct 2026]** The EU has published a set of icons that "creators, publishers and other deployers of generative AI systems may use". Three variants: **Basic icon** (AI was involved, or a custom text label or interactive second layer is used — example given: a "Deepfake video with the text label 'voices generated with' followed by the basic icon"); **Fully AI-Generated** ("the entire deepfake content … is fully generated by AI with no human-created elements or human editorial control (apart from prompting)"); **Partially AI-Modified** ("pre-existing, human-made content was partially modified with AI turning it into a deepfake"). Four colour versions (black, white, and each at 50% transparency), downloadable as SVG and PNG. — [EU icons page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 13 Oct 2026]** Status of the icons: "The use of these EU icons is optional, but the labelling requirements under Article 50 AI Act are not. The use of these icons does not establish legal compliance by itself." Licence: "made publicly available for everyone to use freely, without the need for attribution to the Commission or the AI Office"; use by non-signatories "should not be construed as signaling of their adherence to the code." User testing found that "performance improved across all measures when the basic icon was accompanied by a text label". — [EU icons page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 13 Oct 2026]** Display rules, which the page calls "a summary, not a complete overview" of what Code signatories commit to in Section 2: "The icon should be clearly perceivable and distinguishable at the latest at the time of first exposure"; "placed where no intervening overlay elements exist"; "directly embedded into the deepfake or published text (except for creative works), unless equivalent alternatives are available such as a user interface overlay. The icon must be visible when content is reshared or downloaded." Accessibility advice: clearly visible size; plain-language label with no abbreviations other than "AI"; alt text where possible; "If the disclosure appears for a limited time, it should remain visible long enough to be read and understood". — [EU icons page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-icons-labelling-ai-generated-content)

#### The Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 19 Oct 2026]** Final Code published 10 June 2026 after a process that began in September 2025 (first draft 17 December 2025, second draft 3 March 2026, draft guidelines 8 May 2026). Two sections: "Section 1: Providers - Rules for marking and detection of AI-generated and manipulated content" and "Section 2: Deployers - Rules for labelling of deepfakes and AI-generated and manipulated text". "Even though adherence to the code is voluntary, the transparency requirements under article 50 of the AI Act are legal obligations." — [Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 31 Jul 2026]** Adequacy: "On July 8, the Commission concluded that the Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content adequately covers the obligations provided for in Articles 50(2), (4) and (5) AI Act … The following day, the AI Board adopted its Adequacy Assessment". Limits: "Adherence to the code does not constitute conclusive evidence of compliance with these obligations." Review: "at least every two years". — [Commission opinion on the Code](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-opinion-assessment-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 10 Jun 2026]** "The code does not impose obligations beyond the AI Act"; it "clearly differentiates between measures that are mandatory and required for compliance with the legal obligations in Article 50(2) and (4) of the AI Act and measures that are purely voluntary." Section 2 "provides practical guidance on the design, placement and presentation of labels, disclaimers or icons, while considering specific regimes for artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous works"; Annex I holds the optional EU icon in three variants. — [Commission Q&A: the Code](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 29 Jul 2026]** Not signing is not a breach: "Signing the code of practice is voluntary. Not signing it does not constitute non-compliance with the AI Act." Non-signatories "must be prepared to demonstrate compliance through other adequate means" and "may be subject to a larger number of requests for information". Sections can be signed separately; the form must be signed by "a senior executive with sufficient authority". — [Commission Q&A: Signing the Code](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/signing-code-practice-transparency-ai-generated-content)

#### Enforcement and penalties
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 24 Jul 2026]** "Compliance with the rules will mainly be enforced by national competent market surveillance authorities. The AI Office has a limited role", being competent only for systems built on a general-purpose model by the same provider or integrated into a very large online platform or search engine. "Fines can reach up to 15 million euros or 3% of total worldwide turnover for the preceding financial year, while proportionality can be taken into account in the case of small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) and small mid-cap companies (SMCs)." — [Commission Q&A: Article 50](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 20 Oct 2026]** Penalties are set nationally within EU ceilings: "Member States have to lay down effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties"; "Up to €15m or 3% of the total worldwide annual turnover … for non-compliance with any of the other requirements or obligations of the Regulation"; "For each category of infringement, the threshold would be the lower of the 2 amounts for SMEs and the higher for other companies." — [Commission Q&A: Navigating the AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/navigating-ai-act)
- **[READ DIRECTLY 17 Nov 2026 | page updated 9 Nov 2026]** National enforcement bodies are not all in place. ### Inferences
- **A hobbyist is outside Article 50; a creator who earns from the account is inside it.** Payouts from X's revenue sharing, paid subscriptions, sponsorships, client work or using the clips to promote one's own business all look like "economic benefit on a regular basis" or freelance activity. A creator aiming to make money should assume deployer status from the start. (My reading of the Commission's wording; the Commission's view is interpretive, and a national authority or court could draw the line differently.)
- **For a professional creator, the duty turns on realism, not on "AI-made".** A clearly stylised, cartoon-like or abstract 3D clip does not "falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful", so it is not a deepfake and Article 50(4) requires no label. A photorealistic clip of a real or plausibly real person, place, product or event is a deepfake even when the subject is invented, because "existing" covers what "can plausibly exist".
- **"Clearly artistic or fictional" reduces the duty but does not remove it — if the content is a deepfake at all.** A realistic AI clip that is evidently part of a fictional or artistic piece still needs a disclosure, in a form that does not spoil the work (the commentary's examples: caption, notes, credits). A stylised clip needs none. The exemption question therefore only arises for realistic imagery.
- **A tool's invisible watermark or metadata never discharges the creator's own duty.** The Commission says deployers "cannot simply rely on the machine-readable marking". Where a label is due, it must be something a viewer can see or hear without special tools, present from the first moment of exposure.
- **Safest form where a label is due:** burn a small "AI-generated" text label or the EU icon into the video from the first frame, so that it survives reposting and downloading (the Code's summary says the icon "must be visible when content is reshared or downloaded"). A platform's own label (for example X's "Made with AI" toggle) may count as an "equivalent alternative … such as a user interface overlay", but it disappears when the file is downloaded and reposted elsewhere; whether a platform label alone is enough is not settled by anything I read.
- **Code-driven videos are a special case that mostly falls outside both duties.** When a coding agent writes Remotion or HTML code, the AI system's output is source code, which the Commission lists as outside the provider marking duty; the video itself is rendered by ordinary software. Motion graphics, charts, device mock-ups and 3D text are not deepfakes. The duty would return only if the clip embeds realistic AI-generated images, voices or footage.
- **AI-written post text:** a creator who actually checks the substance of AI-drafted captions and takes responsibility for publishing them fits the human-review exemption; in any case the text duty only covers text "published to inform the public on matters of public interest".
- **Enforcement risk for a solo creator is low in the near term but not zero.** Authorities are still being designated in 11 Member States and missing from the list in three, no enforcement action surfaced, and fines must be proportionate; on the other hand anyone can file a complaint, and the ceiling is high.
- **From 2 December 2026 every generative tool on the EU market must mark its output machine-readably**, so platforms will detect AI origin more often. Removing or stripping marks was not addressed in anything I read; leaving them intact is the cautious course.
- **Using a tool whose maker has not signed the Code is not a problem in itself.** The creator's duty is the same whichever tool is used; signing only changes how the provider proves its own compliance.

## 2. Remotion (www.remotion.dev): licence and price, rendering requirements, CPU-only and integrated-graphics rendering, how 3D scenes render headless, performance guidance, cloud rendering and cost, and official material for AI coding agents

### Takeaway
Remotion is free for any individual and for organisations of up to three people, with all features and commercial use included; larger companies pay $25 per seat per month, or $0.01 per render with a $100 monthly minimum, and enterprise terms start at $500 per month (all monthly billing). It needs only Node.js on 64-bit Windows, macOS 15+ or a recent Linux and renders frame by frame in a headless Chrome it downloads itself, with no graphics card required: 3D scenes built with Three.js need the `angle` graphics setting (pre-set in new projects), which uses an integrated GPU when Chrome can reach it and otherwise falls back to slow software rendering. Remotion publishes no speed benchmark for local rendering; its documented cloud option, AWS Lambda, costs about $0.001–$0.02 for clips of up to a minute, and its official agent material (skills, plugins for five agents, a beginner guide, a prompt gallery) is extensive.

### Inferences
- **Cost for this user is $0 for Remotion itself, permanently, as long as the creator works alone or in a team of up to three.** Monetising the videos or doing client work does not change that.
- **The route is realistic on an integrated-graphics PC.** Rendering is offline and frame-accurate, so a slow machine produces the same video, only later. Ordinary 2D motion graphics are processor-bound ("Most renders do not become faster with GPU acceleration"). On the one integrated-graphics laptop measurement found, a 15–30-second 1080p clip would take roughly 1–2 minutes; other reports suggest up to about 3 minutes for 30 seconds. Treat 1–5 minutes as the planning range for 2D content on a mid-range laptop processor, longer on an old dual-core.
- **3D shots are the uncertain case.** With `angle` (the template default) Chrome can use the Intel or AMD integrated GPU, and light Three.js scenes such as a device mock-up or a few lit meshes should render at broadly similar speed. If Chrome cannot use the GPU it falls back to software rendering, for which the only official statement is "Rendering might be slow". The practical test is one command — `npx remotion gpu --gl=angle` — followed by `npx remotion benchmark` on the actual composition.
- **Two documented fall-backs if headless 3D is slow:** render through the Studio's "Render in browser" button, where "GPU is usually available"; or reduce cost per frame (lower `--scale`, fewer blur and shadow layers, JPEG frames, H.264 output).
- **Cloud rendering is optional and does not make 3D frames cheaper to draw**, because Lambda and Vercel Sandbox have no GPU. Its value is parallelism and freeing the PC. For a solo creator the AWS set-up effort outweighs the saving of a few minutes per clip.
- **Encoding hardware does not matter for this user:** it is software-only on Intel and AMD integrated graphics under Windows, and a 15–30-second H.264 encode is short.
- **Staying on 4.0 versus moving to 5.0 changes nothing material for a solo creator** beyond passing a "free-license" key; the easier WebGL default in 5.0 is a small convenience.

### Gaps
- No official local-rendering benchmark exists, and no benchmark of Three.js scenes on Intel Iris Xe, integrated Arc or Radeon graphics was found. I could not test first-hand.
- Whether Chrome Headless Shell on Windows actually reaches a given integrated GPU through `angle` was not verified; the docs describe the setting, not per-chip behaviour.
- The SocialVik measurement is known only from a search summary: which framework it timed, the composition's content and the date are unconfirmed.
- Prices for Vercel Sandbox, AWS's own Lambda price sheet and free tier, and GitHub Actions' free minutes were not verified (hosts blocked). The Lambda examples were measured on an older Remotion build.
- No release date for Remotion 5.0 is given on the site.
- The currently applicable licence text (`LICENSE.md` on GitHub) was read by the first round, not by me; the site's pricing page and FAQ, read today, agree with it.

### Inferences
- **EEVEE on integrated graphics: plan on a test, not a number.** The honest statement for the report is that no published measurement exists. The reference points bracket the answer loosely: a current mid-range discrete card renders a simple 720p EEVEE frame in about 5 seconds; a 2016 discrete card needed a few seconds to 45 seconds per frame; EEVEE with no working GPU ran at about 50 seconds per frame. A modern integrated GPU (Radeon 780M/890M, Arc 140V) most plausibly lands between a few seconds and a few tens of seconds per 1080p frame for a simple stylised scene, and Iris Xe or older Intel UHD chips slower — an expectation, not a finding.
- **What that means for a 15–30-second clip (360–900 frames):** at 5 seconds per frame, 30–75 minutes; at 30 seconds per frame, 3–7.5 hours; at 60 seconds, 6–15 hours. A 10-frame trial render of the real scene settles which row applies before any commitment.
- **Free rigging is still available; motion capture is the paid part.** Mixamo (humanoids only, unmaintained) or AccuRIG 2 cost nothing. Custom motion from a phone video costs about $18–$20 a month at entry level (Move One Starter, Rokoko Plus) or about $8–$19 a month for Cascadeur's Indie tier; free allowances (DeepMotion, Rokoko Vision) are short and, for DeepMotion, non-commercial.
- **Render-farm cost for a 10–30-second 1080p clip (240–900 frames), my arithmetic on the cited per-frame range:** about $12–$38 for 240 frames and $45–$144 for 900 frames with Cycles at moderate complexity ($0.05–$0.16 per frame); scaling iRender's example ($14 per 200 frames) gives $17–$63. A light, stylised scene should come in below these ranges, EEVEE lower still, and a first short job can fit inside the $25 trial credit at Fox Renderfarm or GarageFarm.
- **SheepIt arithmetic:** ordering costs 10 points per minute of render time and contributing earns 38, so each minute contributed pays for about 3.8 minutes ordered. A 450-frame clip at one minute per frame would need about 4,500 points, or roughly two hours of contributed render time at the reference rate — longer on a slow machine if points follow a reference machine, which I could not confirm. It is free but slow and public.
- **Cloud-PC arithmetic for light use (10 hours a month):** AirGPU's cheapest machine about $6.50 plus $7 storage, so roughly $14 a month before VAT; Vagon Planet about $10 plus its storage fee; Shadow Neo Lite a flat $29.99. A cloud PC also fixes the sluggish local viewport, which a render farm does not.
- **Render farms and cloud PCs remove the hardware limit but not the skills limit.** Modelling, lighting and animating still take the hours and the learning described in the first round.

## 4. Conclusions: the minimum a creator must do under the EU labelling rules, and whether the code-driven Remotion route and the true-3D route are realistic on an integrated-graphics PC, with rough cost and time per finished 15–30-second clip

### Takeaway
Under the EU rules a creator who earns from the account needs to add a visible "AI-generated" disclosure only when a clip is realistic enough to pass as authentic (a deepfake), with a lighter, non-intrusive disclosure allowed when that realistic material sits inside an evidently fictional or artistic piece; stylised 3D and code-driven motion graphics need no label under Article 50, and a purely personal hobbyist is outside the Act. The Remotion route is clearly realistic on an integrated-graphics PC — $0 for the software, about $20 a month for an agent, and minutes of rendering per clip — while the true-3D route is possible but conditional: it costs little money, yet its local render time is unmeasured and it demands hours per clip plus a long learning period.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
**A. The minimum to stay on the right side of the EU labelling rules (inference from the Commission pages; not legal advice)**
1. Decide which side of the personal/professional line you are on. No income and no business purpose: the AI Act does not apply to you, and only platform rules do. Any regular income or business use of the account: treat yourself as a deployer.
2. For each clip, ask one question: could a reasonable viewer in my audience take this for real footage of a real or plausibly real person, place, object or event? If no (stylised, cartoon-like, abstract, obviously impossible, motion graphics), Article 50 requires nothing.
3. If yes, show a clear "AI-generated" label that a viewer sees or hears at the very start, not only at the end and not only in metadata. Burning it into the video (text or the free EU icon, ideally icon plus the words) is the form that survives reposting and downloading.
4. If the realistic material is part of an evidently fictional, artistic or satirical piece, a lighter disclosure is enough (for example a line in the post text or closing credits), but some disclosure must still exist. Do not lean on this for advertising or informative content, where commentators expect a strict reading.
5. If AI writes post text that informs the public on matters of public interest, either review its substance yourself and take responsibility for it, or label it.
6. Do not rely on the generator's invisible watermark to do your job, and leave it intact.
7. Cheap insurance, not a legal requirement: switch on the platform's AI label for anything realistic, and label older realistic content when convenient.

**B. Code-driven Remotion route on an integrated-graphics PC — realistic**
- Money: $0 for Remotion; about $20 a month for a coding agent; rendering locally is free. At 10–20 clips a month that is about $1–$2 per clip. Cloud rendering, if ever wanted, is cents per clip on AWS Lambda but needs an AWS account.
- Time per finished 15–30-second clip: about 10–15 minutes for the agent's first draft (vendor figure), realistically 30 minutes to a few hours with review and revisions, and far more for a highly polished piece; plus about 1–5 minutes of rendering for 2D motion graphics. One-off set-up (Node.js, agent, four commands) is under an hour.
- Caveats: heavy WebGL or Three.js shots depend on Chrome reaching the integrated GPU (check with `npx remotion gpu --gl=angle`); if it cannot, expect a slow software render, for which no official multiplier exists. The look is designed motion graphics and simple 3D (device mock-ups, 3D text, charts, product turntables), not photoreal scenes or characters.

**C. True-3D route (Blender) on an integrated-graphics PC — possible, conditional, and the slowest**
- Money: $0 is achievable (Blender, Mixamo or AccuRIG, local EEVEE or SheepIt). Typical paid extras: $8–$20 a month for an animation or motion-capture helper; rendering either free locally, about $12–$145 per 10–30-second Cycles clip on a commercial farm (often inside the $25 trial for a first short job), or a cloud PC at roughly $14–$30 a month for light use.
- Time per finished 15–30-second clip: several hours of scene work for a new scene (first-round judgement), plus local EEVEE rendering that could be anywhere from about half an hour to more than ten hours because the per-frame time on integrated graphics is unmeasured. Add the learning period: tens of hours to a first decent clip.
- Condition for calling it realistic: a supported integrated GPU, 16 GB of memory, a simple stylised scene, and a 10-frame test render that shows single-digit to low-double-digit seconds per frame. Otherwise the route only works with a cloud PC or a farm, which adds cost and still requires 3D skills.

**D. Ranking for this user:** for fast, cheap, professional-looking output on a weak PC, the code-driven route beats true 3D on money, time and predictability, and it also carries the lightest labelling burden under EU law.

### Gaps
- The time-per-clip figures combine vendor claims, creator anecdotes and one unverified render measurement; no independent, measured end-to-end timing on an integrated-graphics PC exists for either route.
- The minimum-compliance list is my synthesis of Commission guidance that is itself non-binding; the statute, the Code's Section 2 and the Guidelines' full text were not read.
- Platform rules (X, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube), which may demand labels where EU law does not, are covered in the first-round notes, not here.
