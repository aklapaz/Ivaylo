## 1. Copyright ownership: can a creator own or protect an AI-generated video? (US, EU, UK; what level of human contribution matters)

### Takeaway
A video made only by prompting is not protected by copyright in the United States, and that position hardened in 2026: the Supreme Court declined on 2 March 2026 to hear *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, and the Copyright Office's report of 29 January 2025 — read directly on copyright.gov, where it is still the newest guidance and nothing dated 2026 is listed — says prompts alone, however often revised, are not authorship, while a creator's own work that stays perceptible in the output, a creative selection and arrangement of AI material, and creative modifications are protectable case by case. The UK still protects computer-generated works under section 9(3) CDPA, but the government's report of 18 March 2026 proposes repealing it while keeping protection for AI-assisted works (no legislation found); the EU has no statute on the point, applies a human-creative-choices test, and the European Parliament (10 March 2026, non-binding) and German courts say fully AI-generated output is unprotected.

Perlmutter* is listed. — [U.S. Copyright Office: Copyright and Artificial Intelligence](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 29 Jan 2025 · FACT] The Part 2 report's conclusions, verbatim: "Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements"; "Whether human contributions to AI-generated outputs are sufficient to constitute authorship must be analyzed on a case-by-case basis"; "Based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control"; "Human authors are entitled to copyright in their works of authorship that are perceptible in AI-generated outputs, as well as the creative selection, coordination, or arrangement of material in the outputs, or creative modifications of the outputs"; "The use of AI tools to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect the availability of copyright protection for the output"; and "The case has not been made for additional copyright or sui generis protection for AI-generated content." — [Part 2 report (PDF), executive summary](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 29 Jan 2025 · FACT] **Re-rolling does not help:** "Repeatedly revising prompts does not change this analysis"; the effort involved "is irrelevant, as copyright protects original authorship, not hard work"; "No matter how many times a prompt is revised and resubmitted, the final output reflects the user's acceptance of the AI system's interpretation, rather than authorship of the expression it contains." Choosing one output is not authorship either: "selection of a single output is not itself a creative act." The Office adds that a different conclusion "may be called for" if technology later gives users real control over expressive elements. — [Part 2 report (PDF), section II.D](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 29 Jan 2025 · FACT] **What counts as enough human contribution — three routes.** (1) *Expressive inputs:* "where a human inputs their own copyrightable work and that work is perceptible in the output, they will be the author of at least that portion of the output" (the example is a creator's own illustration fed to the model). (2) *Selection and arrangement:* a human may "select, coordinate, and arrange AI-generated material in a creative way", which protects "the output as a whole (although not the AI-generated material alone)"; a footnote warns that arranging only two or three elements is generally not enough. (3) *Modification:* editing AI material "to such a degree that the modifications meet the standard for copyright protection", including with region-by-region editing tools that "enable the user to control the selection and placement of individual creative elements". — [Part 2 report (PDF), sections II.E–F](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 29 Jan 2025 · FACT] **The film example:** "the inclusion of elements of AI-generated content in a larger human-authored work does not affect the copyrightability of the larger human-authored work as a whole. For example, a film that includes AI-generated special effects or background artwork is copyrightable, even if the AI effects and artwork separately are not." The Office also says it "has registered hundreds of works that incorporate AI-generated material, with the registration covering the human author's contribution to the work." — [Part 2 report (PDF)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 29 Jan 2025 · FACT] The Office's own one-sentence summary: outputs of generative AI "can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements. This can include situations where a human-authored work is perceptible in an AI output, or a human makes creative arrangements or modifications of the output, but not the mere provision of prompts." The same release said the Office planned to supplement its March 2023 registration guidance and update its Compendium; no such supplement is listed on the AI page as of 17 Nov 2026. — [Copyright Office NewsNet 1060, 29 Jan 2025](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL · 16 Mar 2023 · FACT] **Registration rule:** applicants "have a duty to disclose" AI-generated content, and "AI-generated content that is more than de minimis should be explicitly excluded from the application" under the "Material Excluded" heading, with a brief description of the human author's contribution. — [Copyright Registration Guidance: Works Containing Material Generated by AI (PDF)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/ai_policy_guidance.pdf)
- [READ DIRECTLY · OFFICIAL court filing · dated 6 Feb 2026 · FACT] **The government's brief in *Allen v. Perlmutter*** (D. Colo., No. 1:24-cv-02665-WJM, Document 58-1). Allen entered prompts "at least 624 times", then altered the Midjourney image with Photoshop and upscaling software and tried to register the whole image. ### Inferences
- **What this creator probably owns in a finished video (US view, strictest of the three):** the script and dialogue, the edit (selection, ordering and timing of clips — provided it is a creative arrangement of more than two or three elements), any human-recorded voice-over, any human-made music or drawings, and hand-made character designs to the extent they remain perceptible in the output. **What the creator probably does not own:** the raw AI-generated clips themselves, however many prompts or re-rolls went into them (the Office's report says re-rolling changes nothing, and its *Allen* brief treats 624 prompts as "ideas"). The Office's own film example fits this creator exactly, in mirror image: a human-made film with AI elements is protected as a whole while the AI elements alone are not.
- **The strongest lever a creator controls is the "expressive input" route:** drawing or modelling the character first (even roughly) and using it as the reference image gives a claim to the parts of that design that stay perceptible in the output — something text-only prompting never does. This is the Office's stated position, not yet tested in court for video.
- **Practical consequence a newcomer misses:** someone who lifts a bare AI clip (not the whole edited video) may be infringing nothing in the US. The more human-authored layers the video has, the more of it is protectable — which lines up with what the platforms' "inauthentic content" rules reward anyway (round-one notes, Q6).
- **Keep evidence of human authorship:** dated scripts, storyboards, reference sketches, project files and prompt logs. This matters for a US registration (AI parts must be disclosed and disclaimed) and for any dispute over who copied whom.
- **Regional summary.** US: no protection for purely AI-generated material; settled at appellate level, with the AI-assisted threshold still open (*Allen* pending). UK: purely computer-generated works are, for now, still protected by s 9(3), but the government has proposed repeal and nothing found shows a bill — a creator should not build on it. EU/EEA: no statute; national courts (Germany) refuse protection for prompt-only output and require identifiable human shaping; AI-assisted work with real human creative choices can qualify.
- The law is **unsettled exactly where this creator works** — AI clips plus human editing, sound and writing. None of the three jurisdictions has a decided case on an AI-assembled video.

## 2. Intellectual-property risk for ordinary users (studio styles, famous characters, logos, real people's likeness and voice; what the studio lawsuits mean for users; documented action against individual creators; takedowns)

### Takeaway
No case of an individual creator being sued over AI-generated output was found. What is documented is platform-level action: YouTube removed dozens of AI videos of Disney characters after a Disney legal letter (December 2025), and demonetised and then terminated two large AI "fake trailer" channels (2025). The studio lawsuits target vendors and are unresolved in the latest reports (Midjourney: no settlement, ruling or trial date, discovery still running in October 2026; MiniMax: motion to dismiss denied; ByteDance/Seedance: cease-and-desist letters only, no suit found as of July 2026); for users they translate into tighter prompt filters, halted or regional rollouts, vanished licensed options (Sora) and takedowns — while a federal US likeness-and-voice right (NO FAKES Act) is still not law.

### Inferences
- **Who gets sued and who gets taken down.** On everything found, studios sue *vendors* and send link lists to *platforms*; the individual creator's realistic exposure is removal of the video, loss of monetisation and, for a pattern of it, loss of the channel — not a lawsuit. That is an observation about 2025–2026 practice, not a legal shield: publishing an infringing character is still infringement by the person who publishes it, and the terms of every tool checked put that responsibility on the user.
- **A risk ladder for prompts (inference; no source addressed "style" directly):**
  1. *Describing a look* (soft subsurface skin, big expressive eyes, stylised proportions, glossy toy materials, cinematic rim light) — lowest risk; nothing in the disputes found targets a generic 3D-animation look.
  2. *Naming a studio in the prompt* ("Pixar style", "Disney style") — the cases found complain about recognisable characters, not style words, but naming a studio increases the chance the model reproduces protected character designs and is increasingly caught by filters added after the 2026 disputes.
  3. *Using a studio or film name in the title, caption or hashtags* — this is where the fake-trailer channels were hit, under misleading-metadata rules rather than copyright. Never label a video as if it came from a studio.
  4. *Recognisable characters, logos or mascots* — documented takedowns (Disney/YouTube); also the heart of the pending suits.
  5. *Real people's faces or voices* — platform bans on fake endorsements (round-one notes), vendor filters, state publicity laws in the US, the EU deepfake-disclosure duty (round-one notes), and a federal US right that is pending but stalled.
- **What the lawsuits change for users in practice:** (a) filters — prompts and reference images with famous characters or real faces get blocked more often, sometimes over-blocking innocent prompts; (b) availability — Seedance 2.0's global rollout was halted for a time, and a model or feature a workflow depends on can be withdrawn or region-limited at short notice; (c) no safe licensed alternative — the one mainstream licensed-character route (Sora) is gone; (d) evidence trails — the October 2026 order on Midjourney's video training data shows the output-focused case is moving toward the merits, and a judgment or settlement in 2027 could change Midjourney's video product.
- **For this creator** the cheap, durable choice is an original character and world described in neutral visual terms; it avoids every rung above the first and is also what makes the work protectable (Q1) and "authentic" for monetisation (round-one notes).

  5. "You own the output" in a vendor's terms cannot create a copyright that the law denies (Q1); it only means the vendor does not claim it.

## 4. Editing on integrated graphics (DaVinci Resolve, Clipchamp, Canva, Descript, Instagram Edits, Shotcut, Kdenlive; browser- and phone-based options)

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- **When native audio is enough (inference; no measured evidence found):** for a single-clip post — one shot with ambience, effects and perhaps a line of speech — native audio is the fastest and cheapest option, since it is included in the generation. It stops being enough when several generated clips are cut together: each clip gets its own voice, music and room tone, so the edit needs one continuous music bed, one consistent voice and a levelled mix added in the editor. A sensible default is to generate with native audio on, keep the effects and ambience, and replace music and narration.
- **Lowest-cost audio stack for a monetised, cross-posted video:** ElevenLabs Starter (about $6/month) for voice and effects, plus a free music source whose licence is not tied to one platform (YouTube Audio Library or Pixabay) or a paid AI-music plan (round-one notes). Budget $0 only if the videos use native audio or no voice.
- **The cross-posting trap:** a video cut to a trending TikTok or Instagram sound cannot be legally re-posted to X or YouTube with that audio. Since X is the primary platform and has no music library of its own (unverified background), the master file should carry independently licensed audio, and platform sounds should be avoided altogether.
- **What $6 buys on ElevenLabs Starter (my arithmetic from the directly read rates; 30,000 credits = $0.0002 per credit):** a 60-second narration of about 900 characters ≈ 900 credits ≈ $0.18; a sound effect ≈ $0.04; a minute of music ≈ $0.18 at the FAQ rate. A month of eight 30–60-second videos with narration, ten effects each and a music bed uses roughly 25,000–30,000 credits — Starter is about the right size, with no headroom for lip-sync or upscaling.
- **Lip-sync:** at ElevenLabs' "from 67 credits per second", a 30-second talking shot is about 2,000 credits (≈ $0.40 on Starter) — far cheaper than the $1.50 (at $0.05/second) to $4.50 (at $0.15/second) implied by the specialist tools' third-party prices. Narration over characters whose mouths are not the focus, or models that generate speech natively, avoids the cost altogether.
- ElevenLabs' licence over user content is as broad as the CapCut one flagged in round one (perpetual, irrevocable, sub-licensable, and usable to develop new products). The practical caution is the same: do not upload a real person's voice you have no rights to, and assume scripts and outputs are not confidential.
- The commercial right depends on *being a paid user when the service is used*. The terms read directly do not say what happens to audio generated on the free plan if the user later upgrades; the safe reading is that free-tier audio stays non-commercial, so regenerate it after subscribing.

## 7. Audience and algorithm reaction (2025–2026 surveys on AI video and "AI slop"; measured effects of AI labels; platform actions; evidence-based practices)

### Takeaway
Audiences are wary rather than uniformly hostile: 2026 surveys show most people have noticed suspected AI video, about a third say it lowers their opinion of a brand while about a third trust it as much as human-made video, and rejection is strongest for AI-written or AI-acted storytelling. The best 2026 experiment on labels (Copenhagen Business School) found large drops in emotional engagement when a post is labelled AI-generated, strongest for emotional content, while other studies found little change in likes or shares — and none measured algorithmic reach. Platforms are responding mainly with labels and user-side "see less AI" controls (TikTok, Pinterest), a LinkedIn report button and YouTube's enforcement, not by removing AI content as such.

### Inferences
- **The label is not the main problem; being read as effortless is.** The Frontiers result (an AI label lowers perceived effort) and the CBS result (the penalty is largest for emotional content) point the same way as the surveys (AI-written and AI-acted stories are rejected most; AI as a production aid is tolerated). The practices this supports:
  1. Make the *human* contribution visible — a written story or joke, a recognisable narrator or point of view, deliberate editing and sound design.
  2. Use AI for the visuals of a concept that is the creator's own, rather than for content that asks viewers for an emotional response to something they suspect nobody made.
  3. Show the process occasionally (prompt-to-final breakdowns, character sheets): it answers the "no effort" assumption directly. (Inference; no study tested this.)
  4. Disclose plainly. Large majorities say they want disclosure (86% in one global study), viewers already suspect AI in most cases (83% have noticed it), and concealment adds a trust penalty to the label penalty when it is discovered.
  5. Stylised 3D is a comparatively safe genre: nobody is deceived about reality, mandatory labels mostly do not apply (round-one notes), and the "is this real?" backlash attaches to photoreal content.
- **Expect a real engagement cost from labels on emotional storytelling, and little on informative or playful content** — that is the pattern in the one experiment that separated them. A creator choosing formats could favour humour, craft showcases, explainers and product-style shots over sentimental narrative.
- **User-side controls cap reach** for content that classifiers tag as AI on TikTok and Pinterest: part of the audience has opted down. Combined with the Kapwing figure (59% of a new TikTok feed), the market is saturated with low-effort AI video; volume is the wrong strategy, distinctiveness the right one.
- Evidence quality is uneven: the surveys are mostly commercial or small panels; the experiments use static posts or simulated feeds, not live short-video platforms. Treat magnitudes as indicative.
